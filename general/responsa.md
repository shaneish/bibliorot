Good morning Paster Mike,

I want to start by saying I truly *loved* your sermon on Qohelet.  *Truly*.  Ecclesiastes specifically is my favorite book from the Hebrew bible and has had a profound impact on my beliefs and relationship with God.  I still remember the deep sense of connection I felt reading Qohelet 1:7 for the first time, "All rivers go to the sea, and the sea is not full."  Robert Alther's translation of Ecclesiastes is by far my favorite (as is true for most of the Hebrew Bible), so I could barely contain my excitement when you translated "havel" as "merest breath" (although I do have a soft spot for how the CEB retains the alliteration of "havel havalim" in 1:2 by using "perfectly pointless")

The intent of this email is really twofold, 1) I'm hoping to find out if you have study group available for those wishing to study and discuss the sermons (and the Word more generally) under your guidance, and 2) I feel called to offer my experience as an ex-atheist to elucidate some more effective methods of outreach to non-believers.  I know that second point doesn't sound related to Ecclesiastes, but Ecclesiastes was *the* work that brought me in to a relationship with God.  I think outreach centered on the heart of Ecclesiastes could have a number of advantages over traditional apologetics approaches.

I had considered emailing you after the previous series on non-believers, but didn't want to come off as critical -- especially since I did enjoy the series and there was real value there.  However, given the role Ecclesiastes played in my conversion, the timing of this series on Ecclesiastes shortly afterwards, and [a very coincidentally-timed Reddit comment I made](https://www.reddit.com/r/physicsmemes/comments/1nqsgr4/comment/ngjhq5b) on this exact topic *the day before* your sermon, I feel that God may be nudging me a bit to reach out.  Your emphasis in sermon of Qohelet's double meaning as "one who assembles" truly sent shivers down my spine; I felt God was speaking directly to me through you with that line.  Between that and your emphasis on the foundational role God plays giving *meaning* to our lives, it gave serious weight to my thoughts that Qohelet's wisdom provides valuable tools to help grow ("assemble") the church with non-believers -- because (most) non-believers are searching for the same thing as everyone else, *meaning*.

To give some quick context here, I grew up in a church-going family (grandfather was a Methodist pastor) but always had doubts about Christianity as it was taught to me.  I was only 11 years old when I decided it was all fantasy and became a strong atheist (I have vivid memories of that *exact* moment to this day).  It wasn't until I was 34 that my perspective began to switch and, after 3 years of daily prayer, attending church, and wrestling with my beliefs, I was baptized on Easter of my 37th birthday at ACC.  My linked Reddit comment above has a few more details on the process, but the long and short of it is that I began to see hints of the underlying influence and value of Christian thought (and mythology more generally) from reading epic fantasy novels of all things.  This led me to go back and reread the bible with fresh eyes, and it was in Ecclesiastes (Job as well to a lesser extent) where I found a story that truly spoke to the unsatisfied whisper that has haunted my heart since childhood.  Here was a narrative that pulled no punches, called it like it is, and gave me a (very unsatisfying at the time) hint of why I was never satisfied with any self-constructed meaning in my life.

If I hadn't already puzzled over the "unsolved question" of our innate human predisposition to religious thinking (in retrospect, this question was solved thousands of years ago and I simply neglected the answer) or the odd results from real, peer reviewed studies showing physical/mental benefits of religious practice beyond community alone, I may have simply written Solomon's words of entirely.  If I hadn't serendipitously opened my bible to Ecclesiastes to explore textual references made in the book I was reading at the time (*The Name of the Wind* by Patrick Rothfuss -- ironically an agnostic himself), I may have neglected to even read Ecclesiastes at all.  But, as it were, God had been guiding me to that moment for a long time, so when I started reading those beautiful words they struck me like the guitar cords of the most beautiful song of songs.

Beyond the answer of meaning, Solomon's warning that seeking too many answers too quickly can hinder your development was the perfect advice for beginning my journey and perfectly timed at the end of the book.  It was advice I'd go back to multiple times as I more deeply explored a relationship with Jesus, as that relationship is built on *a lot* of information -- including information which seems wholly unbelievable to my lived experience.  It helped remind me that I didn't need all the answers up front -- I didn't need to understand/interpret the impossibilities of the resurrection, I didn't need to confront the conflicting information I found throughout the bible or the inconsistent depictions of the afterlife.  Rather than being held "making many books" and "much chatter," I could simply begin to pray and contemplate my interpretion of the text and traditions in their own time.  I have found that a lot of those questions are tangential to the important stuff anyways.

Speaking of which, I might be going off on a tangent now, I just think it helps to illustrate the hole in my heart and *why* Ecclesiastes -- not "propositional apologetics" -- was an effective fill.  You're probably asking how generalizable this is to other non-believers, so now it's time to address that.  The long and short of it is that discussions I've had with others about their experiences suggests my experience is not uncommon -- given the topic and ideas are approached with the right attitude, interpretive framework, and *without* dogmatic barriers (the last is particularly important as it's a big reason a lot of people -- myself included -- abandon religion to begin with).

To address the substance of "the hole" and how non-believers are not immune to it, we need to look no further than the panel ACC held with non-believers.  While not being directly address, the questions asked of the panels did a surprisingly good job of illustrating the heart of the issue.  Each non-believer struck on "the hole" in their own subtle ways, but having spent a lot of time contemplating why conversion I was on the lookout for them from the beginning.  The diversity of non-belief on the panel was surprisingly representative of my personal experience with non-believers (of which I have *a lot*, the vast majority of my close personal friends fell into, or are still in, the same camp).  Beyond that, in disposition of each panelist I recognized a distinct "phase" of my life where I'd felt the same way.  In my notes I even wrote "interestingly, after the initial atheism phase I went Omar -> Jenny -> Christine (w/ a sprinkle of Matt thrown in)" lol.  There was no "militant atheist" on the panel, but those have become surprisingly less common in the last decade or so.

Each of the panelists expressed the feelings and/or action of a non-believer looking for...*something*...and unable to find it.  Matt was the most direct example of this, as he was clearly looking for a religious practice of some kind but was unable to rationally settle himself on a single one (a bias against bias I had to overcome myself).  Even more than that, his acknowledgement that giving up Christianity gave him a responsibility for finding meaning hints that he *is* looking for meaning -- but cannot get over the incompatibilist dogma a lot of Protestants are brought up with.

Similarly with Jenny, she shared a number of experiences which reverberate with my history of seeking meaning and purpose -- she even shared experiences that made me think her parents were as well, such as taking her to Christmas services for the experience despite not being believers.  Her openness to religious practice is a big one, I spent many years saying "I *wish* I could make myself believe" and it feels like Jenny is at that same point.  Christine's quest through self-help books, workout routines, diets, gratitude journals, and more once again reverberates with my experience -- I went through the same phase, desperately searching for an answer to the question I hadn't even considered.

These are people desperately searching for a way to fill the God-sized hole in their heart, but they don't know the hole is "God-sized" and they haven't been equipped with a methodological framework for interpreting scripture/tradition in a way consistent with (and complementary to) the wider world of established scientific knowledge.  What they need is the nudge to understand what they seek, reassurance that you don't need all the answers, and a community to support them as they *wrestle with scripture* -- free of the condemnation and vilification that comes from asking the wrong question in front of the wrong person[^1].  Traditional apologetics is counter-productive in this respect, it is oppositional by nature and built on the presupposition that there is one, clear interpretive lens for scripture.

No argument about the first mover, objective morality, or the fossil record will help a non-believer to find Christ; all it will do is push them in the opposite direction, away from people so sure of themselves.  Even today I find arguments about the first mover and objective morality unconvincing -- not because I don't believe in a first mover or objective mortality (I do), but because I can just as easily conceive a secular response to such challenges.

Thomas Aquinas spent his entire life writing "logical" defenses of God (including the "unmoved mover" argument you used in a previous sermon), yet after his divine experience said "I can write no more, I have seen things that make my writings like straw."  God is not bound by propositions.  No propositional formula will reveal the divine to non-believers -- just like the writings of Aquinas, apologetics built on "rational" arguments is but straw to the ears of non-believers.  You must speak to the missing meaning in their heart (the God-sized hole) and help them to see that science and religion are complementary tools for investigating orthogonal planes of truth -- the material and experiential.  Both of which bring us closer to the Lord.

There *are* a lot of gaps and unexplained/weird observations in the established materialist model of our world that can help guide people to seeing where and how God can fit into the picture, but these do not establish evidence *for* God in the apologetic sense.  Questions like:
- why do humans seem to have an innate sense of the divine?
- why is religious belief so universal among human cultures?
- why are so many non-believers spiritual? (as an aside, this strikes back to a funny ["are there no atheists left?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9WnOYHi5k4g&list=PLAKNnDKIWa7pGT0U_gEKzSGoWCI46k_X5&index=50&t=3314s) joke in a believer vs atheist debate where we find out all the atheists are going to church)
- why are humans so naturally drawn to finding *meaning* in the world around us?
- why do we feel a sense of awe from our world and place in the cosmos?
- why do we have conscious experience?
- what is conscious experience?
- where does conscious experience come from?
- what is identity and how does it contain continuity?
These are all questions which don't have solid, established material explanation or basis and can hint to the materialist that there might be something more happening than they think at first -- but to be effective, these are questions individuals must grapple with themselves or we risk them being dismissed up front. (note: there are a lot of other little questions and observations like this that have, over the years, hinted to me that something was missing from my purely-materialistic perspective -- but the questions above are within the zeitgeist of modern scientific discussion and more likely to be relatable).

Apologetic arguments are havel.  Havel, havel, havel.  There is a reason so many non-believers label themselves "spiritual" -- they *are* looking for God, they just don't know it yet.  When you ask the right questions -- in a setting unafraid of- and unjudging in- the response -- God's word defends itself.

Sorry, that was a lot, but I've been thinking about this a lot for a long time now.  Would be interested to hear your thoughts.

Thanks,
Shane Stephenson

[^1]: In a way, this is more a Protestant problem more than a Christianity problem.  We currently view the rise in Catholic converts under the lense of conservatism, but more quietly the Catholic church has also been capturing the return of left-leaning ex-nonbelievers as well.  I think this comes down to something you've hit on multiple times in sermon: the position of good works and actions within the Christian identity.  When you believe that salvation in Jesus Christ comes through *faith alone*, the specific extra-biblical dogma which defines what you must have faith in becomes the sole measuring stick for considering who is or is not Christian.  When you emphasize actions/works and not just belief alone (like the Catholic church does), there is more tolerance to variance in scriptural interpretation when it comes to deciding if someone else is a Christian or not.  I think it no coincidence the Catholic clergy has produced some of the greatest advancements in science the last few centuries -- Catholic priests have given us everything from the foundation of genetics (Gregor Mendel) to the big bang itself (Georges Lemaitre).  Catholics do not need to worry about the infighting and politics that come from strict scriptural interpretation requirements, whether you believe Genesis was metaphorical or literal isn't nearly as important to your Christian identity as your actions are -- whether you attend mass, pray daily, and try to live up to the example of Jesus in your own life.  This problem with mainline Protestant thinking is one of the big reasons I've appreciated your sermons, as more than once you've emphasized that our actions *are* important as Christians.
